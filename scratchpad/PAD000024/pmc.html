Abstract

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent neuropsychiatric disorder associated with significant morbidity and mortality. Increasing evidence suggests that a subset of MDD patients exhibit a dysregulated immune system. However, few clinical studies have tested the efficacy of anti-inflammatory drugs in reducing symptoms of depression. In contrast, targeted immunomodulatory drugs have revolutionized the treatment of inflammatory skin disorders, such as atopic dermatitis (AD) and psoriasis. To assess the viability of a targeted treatment approach in MDD, we first compared the blood proteomic profiles of patients with MDD to those of patients with AD, psoriasis, and healthy controls (HCs). We demonstrated that the proteomic signatures of MDD patients share Th2 skewing and dysregulation of other immune/neurovascular-related proteins with AD. Next, we performed an in-silico drug repurposing analysis to test whether common biologics used in dermatology could also affect the dysregulated proteomic signature observed in MDD patients. This computational approach identified dupilumab, which targets the IL-4 receptor α subunit (IL-4Rα) and thus inhibits the Th2 axis, as significantly affecting the MDD signature by reversing the dysregulation of several inflammatory proteins related to Th2 signaling. Finally, in a mouse model of chronic social defeat stress (CSDS), we showed that pharmacological inhibition of IL-4Rα prevented stress-induced social avoidance behavior. Our findings underscore the potential role of the Th2 axis in MDD, highlighting the potential of specifically targeting Th2 as a disease-modifying treatment. Additionally, the back-translational drug repurposing strategy employed in this study may offer a novel approach to identify immunomodulatory drugs in psychiatry.

INTRODUCTION

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent neuropsychiatric disorder with significant morbidity and mortality, due to a risk for suicide and various comorbid medical conditions like cardiovascular disorders and cancer [1]. Despite the availability of effective pharmacological therapies for MDD, more than a third of patients do not achieve full remission following treatment with standard antidepressant medications [2]. One of the many reasons for this insufficient treatment outcome is our limited understanding of the underlying pathological mechanisms of MDD [3].

There is increasing evidence of a close interaction between the brain and immune system contributing to the pathophysiology of stress-related disorders, including MDD [4]. Pre-clinical animal models have shown that various forms of stress lead to changes in circulating immune cells and secreted proteins and that blocking specific targets can prevent stress-induced behavioral changes [5–7]. In humans, it is now well-established that approximately 20–30% of patients with MDD show a blood signature with features of low-grade inflammation. Interleukin (IL-) 6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF-) α, and C-reactive protein (CRP) have been reported to be elevated among MDD patients compared to healthy individuals [8]. On a cellular level, increases in total leukocytes and various cells of both myeloid (e.g., neutrophils and monocytes) and lymphoid lineages (e.g., T-helper cells) have been reported [9].

Despite the large number of studies describing immune changes in MDD, few clinical studies have tested the efficacy of anti-inflammatory drugs in reducing symptoms of depression. In fact, infliximab, a TNF-α inhibitor, is one of the few biologics that has been tested directly in patients with primary mood disorders, with evidence of greater benefit for depression in those with higher circulating levels of CRP [10] and lipid markers [11]. Thus, despite these growing insights into immunological alterations in MDD, the therapeutic role of immunomodulation has been largely unexplored.

This stands in stark contrast to primary immune-inflammatory disorders like atopic dermatitis (AD) or psoriasis. AD is the most common inflammatory skin disorder affecting up to 10% of adults [12], characterized by pruritic and erythematous lesions commonly affecting the flexor surfaces, face, and scalp [13]. Psoriasis, which affects about 3% of the population [14], characteristically presents with well-demarcated plaques with silvery white scales, accompanied by pruritus and nail changes. Dysregulation of T-helper cells is a key feature of both AD and psoriasis [15–18]. In recent years, several immunomodulatory therapies have been approved for these disorders. Most prominently, dupilumab, which targets the IL-4 receptor α subunit (IL-4Rα) and thus inhibits the Th2 pathway, was the first monoclonal antibody approved as a long-term therapy for the treatment of moderate-to-severe AD [19–21]. Dupilumab has demonstrated clinical efficacy, reversal of the inflamed molecular signature of AD, and a favorable safety profile, even receiving FDA approval in children as young as 6 months of age for the treatment of moderate-to-severe AD [19, 20, 22–25]. Clinical trial, open label, and real-world data show that the most common adverse events of dupilumab include ocular symptoms (e.g. conjunctivitis), injection site reactions, and nasopharyngitis, while the rates of serious adverse events were comparable to placebo [19, 23–26]. The approved treatments for psoriasis largely target the Th17/IL-23 axis, including inhibitors of IL-17A (e.g. secukinumab), IL-23p19 (e.g. risankizumab), and IL-12/IL-23 p40 (e.g. ustekinumab). These targeted therapies have been shown to downregulate key Th17 and Th1-related markers in the skin and serum [27–29].

Given the successful translational approach in dermatology, where disease immunophenotyping led to advances in understanding pathogenesis and expansion of the therapeutic pipeline, the systemic immune activation in MDD could potentially be similarly leveraged to facilitate the development of more specific and effective treatments. We therefore performed in-depth proteomic profiling to compare the serum inflammatory signature in patients with MDD, AD, psoriasis, and healthy controls (HCs). Using an in-silico approach, we also implemented a drug improvement analysis, testing whether common biologics used in dermatology may also impact the observed dysregulated MDD proteomic signature [30]. Because the MDD signature showed Th2 dysregulation and significant improvement with dupilumab, using a mouse model of chronic social defeat stress (CSDS), we hypothesized that pharmacologically blocking Th2 by neutralizing circulating IL-4Rα with a monoclonal antibody may prevent social stress-induced behavioral alterations. Overall, our data support the potential pathogenicity of the Th2 axis in MDD, warranting further exploration of specific immune targeting of Th2 as a novel, disease-modulating treatment strategy. Furthermore, the applied back-translational drug repurposing strategy could be applied to other disease conditions and medications.

METHODS

Patient characteristics

MDD was assessed by the Structured Clinical Interview for the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders–Fifth Edition (SCID-5) [31]. Participants with MDD completed the Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology-Self Report (QIDS-SR) to measure depressive symptom severity [32]. Out of 25 patients with MDD, two were on concurrent antidepressant treatment. AD and psoriasis patients were recruited from outpatient dermatology clinics in Mount Sinai and all had a dermatologist-confirmed diagnosis with symptoms lasting >1 year. All AD and psoriasis patients were moderate-to-severe with body surface area of >10%. Disease severity was further characterized using SCORing of Atopic Dermatitis (SCORAD) [33] and Psoriasis Activity Scoring Index (PASI) [34] for AD and psoriasis, respectively.

MDD and HC subjects had no history of atopy or co-occurring primary inflammatory disorders. AD, psoriasis, and HC subjects had no prior clinical diagnosis of MDD. Other exclusion criteria applying to all enrolled subjects included active infection, current substances of abuse, other unstable medical illnesses, history of immunosuppression, and concomitant use of anti-inflammatory medications. AD and psoriasis subjects were specifically screened to exclude the use of systemic steroids, immunosuppressants, or phototherapy within 4 weeks of sample collection and the use of topical steroids or calcineurin inhibitors within 2 weeks of sample collection.

Blood samples

On the day of the blood draw, patients were fasted for at least 6 h. Blood was drawn into Vacutainer Gold Top 5 mL Silica Gel tubes (BD, #365968). Blood was allowed to clot for >30 min, centrifuged at 1300 g for 15 min at 4 °C, then aliquoted and stored at −80 °C. Serum aliquots from subjects were analyzed using the high-throughput proteomic Olink Proseek multiplex assay (Olink Proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden), as previously described [15, 16, 35]. Serum levels of 353 proteins derived from four panels (General Inflammation, Neurological Inflammation, Cardiovascular Disease II, Cardiovascular Disease III) were quantified.

Mouse chronic social defeat stress (CSDS) and social interaction (SI) test

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Seven-week-old C57BL/6 J (Stock#: 000664) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. Male CD-1 retired breeders (4–6-months-old; Charles River Laboratories, Crl:CD1[ICR]) were used as aggressors for male CSDS. Mice were habituated to the animal facility for at least 1 week. Animals were maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 7am, lights off at 7 pm) with ad libitum access to food and water. For the social interaction (SI) test, mice were allowed to acclimate to the testing room for at least 1 h. Before each defeat, CD-1 mice were screened for aggressive behavior for three consecutive days based on previously described criteria [36]. Two days before the start of the defeat, CD-1 mice were housed on one side of a perforated Plexiglas partition. During 10 consecutive days of CSDS, experimental mice (7–8-week-old) were subjected to direct physical interaction with a CD-1 mouse for 10 min per day and housed on the other side of the Plexiglas divider for the remaining 24 h, allowing for sensory but not direct physical contact. Experimental mice received 8 μg of rat anti-mouse IL-4R antibody (CD124, clone: mIL4R-M1 [RUO], BD Biosciences, #552288) or isotype control rat anti-mouse IgG2a κ (clone: R35–95, Fisher Scientific, # 554687) daily throughout the CSDS paradigm, or during the 10 days of the control experiment. In the defeated mice, antibodies were administered intraperitoneally 20 min before the defeat bout. After the last day of defeat, stressed and unstressed control mice were singly housed. All stressed mice were carefully examined for wounding during the CSDS experiments and mice with excessive wounding were excluded. The SI test was performed 24 h after the last defeat session. After a 1-h habituation period to the behavioral suite (red light conditions), mice were placed into a Plexiglas arena (42 cm3, Nationwide Plastics) with a small, meshed enclosure on one end. For the first 2.5 min, the experimental mouse freely explored the arena. The mouse was then removed from the arena, which was subsequently cleaned with 70% ethanol. Then, a novel social target (CD-1 mouse) was placed into the enclosure and the experimental mouse was placed back into the arena for another 2.5 min. Locomotor activity was tracked and recorded using a Noldus Ethovision System (Noldus Information Technology Inc, Version 11.0, Leesburg, VA). SI ratio was calculated as the ratio between the time the experimental mouse spent in the vicinity of the enclosure (SI zone) when a target mouse was present versus absent. Mice with an SI ratio of ≥1 show a behavioral profile similar to unstressed control mice and were termed “resilient,” while mice with an SI ratio <1 were termed “susceptible.”

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed with R language (www.R-project.org) using publicly available Bioconductor packages (www.bioconductor.org). Fold changes (FCs) were estimated, and hypothesis testing was conducted using contrasts under the general framework for linear models using the limma package, adjusting for age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) as covariates. P-values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control for false discovery rate (FDR) are available in Supplementary Table 1. Proteins with |FC | > 1.2 and P < 0.05 or FDR < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Pathway analyses were performed using the GSVA package. Correlations with clinical parameters were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient analysis.

Publicly available microarray datasets of AD clinical trials were analyzed as previously described [30]. Expression values were modeled with mixed-effect models, with treatment and time as fixed factors and a random effect for each patient. Drug improvement percentages were calculated at available timepoints by the following formula: −100 * (ExpLS.WEEK.X – ExpLS.WEEK.0)/(ExpLS.WEEK.0 – ExpNL.WEEK.0). This analysis was performed with the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) as above and with differentially expressed genes derived from published whole-blood RNA-sequencing datasets [37, 38]. Statistical analyses of the mouse studies were performed with GraphPad Prism software (Version 9, GraphPad Software Inc.). For mouse experiments, outliers were identified using the Grubbs’ test and excluded from analyses.

RESULTS

We enrolled 25 patients with MDD, 30 with AD, 21 with psoriasis, and 32 HCs (Fig. 1A). No significant differences were noted in age, sex, race, or BMI among groups (Table 1). To characterize the systemic immune changes of MDD in comparison to inflammatory skin conditions, we used the Olink proteomic assay to measure 353 serum proteins in patients with MDD, AD, psoriasis, and HCs. We identified 77 (64 up, 13 down) DEPs in MDD, 88 (87 up, 1 down) in AD, and 98 (84 up, 4 down) in psoriasis (Supplementary Figure 1, Supplementary Table 1). The numbers of unique DEPs in MDD, AD, and psoriasis, were 45 (32 up, 13 down), 31 (30 up, 1 down), and 43 (39 up, 4 down), respectively. MDD shared 23 up-regulated proteins with AD and 21 with psoriasis.

MDD proteomic signature shows both unique and common features with AD and psoriasis

The proteins with the most pronounced dysregulation in either MDD, AD, or psoriasis compared to controls are depicted in an unsupervised hierarchical clustering heatmap (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table 1). Broadly, the clustering algorithm divided the markers by disease category into three groups, suggesting that MDD, AD, and psoriasis each harbor distinct proteomic features.

Proteins with unique or preferential dysregulation in MDD included those related to activation of T-cells and other immune cells (CD244, CD40, LAT), Th17 differentiation (CXCL1), negative immune regulation (CD274, GAL8), apoptosis (CASP3, STK4), inflammasome activity modulation (STAMBP), and lipid metabolism (MANF, DECR1, NAAA). The unique MDD signature also encompassed markers with previously implicated roles in neuronal proliferation and differentiation (TGM2, AXIN1) [39–41], neural inflammation and neurodegeneration (SIRT2, IKBKG) [42–45], and metabolism of potentially neurotoxic products (GLO1) [46]. Additionally, several proteins that promote vascular inflammation and angiogenesis (PDGFB, CXCL5, PECAM1, VWF, F2R, F11R, LRPAP1, ITGB1BP2), with possible roles in atherosclerotic plaque development and/or cardiovascular disease [47–50], were also increased in MDD, but not AD or psoriasis.

Although MDD serum demonstrated a distinct inflammatory signature with abnormalities that were not detected in AD or psoriasis, several dysregulated proteins were also shared between MDD and the inflammatory skin diseases. The Th2 chemokines CCL13 and CCL17, which predictably were most dysregulated in AD, were also significantly increased in MDD (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table 1). Th1 markers (CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11) were increased in all three diseases, with CXCL11 showing the highest FCs in MDD, while CXCL9 and CXCL10 were higher in AD and psoriasis. Several markers of T-cell activation that have also been implicated in atherosclerosis were elevated either in all three conditions (CD40LG, TNFSF14/LIGHT) or in MDD and AD (IL-16) [17, 35]. Other protein increases that MDD shared with AD and/or psoriasis included KYNU, which promotes Th17 differentiation, CASP8, which is pro-apoptotic, and several markers implicated in angiogenesis and/or neuronal development/function (SELP, CLEC1B, SRC, PLAT, PLXNB3, NMNAT1) [51–55]. An additional subset of markers was dysregulated in the inflammatory skin conditions but not in MDD (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table 1).

The shared immune signature between MDD and inflammatory skin diseases was further reinforced by pathway enrichment analyses using previously published immune-related datasets (Fig. 2) [56–58]. MDD, AD, and psoriasis all demonstrated significant enrichment in proteins related to CD4+ T-cells and Th2 activation (Fig. 2A, C). All three conditions also showed up-regulation in proteins that are inducible by IFN-α, IFN-γ, and IL-17/TNF-α (Fig. 2B, D, E).

Spearman correlation coefficient analyses were performed to assess the association between serum markers and QIDS-SR (Supplementary Table 2). QIDS-SR demonstrated a strong positive correlation with ACP5, whose up-regulation in neurons has been linked to depression-like behavior in mice [59]. Additionally, Th1 markers (IFN-γ, CXCL10), innate immunity cytokine IL-2, and growth factor FGF21 were also positively correlated with QIDS-SR.

In-silico analysis demonstrates down-regulation of the MDD serum inflammatory signature by dupilumab and other systemic therapeutics

For AD, several systemic therapies have been evaluated in clinical trials in patients with moderate-to-severe disease, with accompanying mechanistic studies that measured molecular changes in the skin before and after treatment [16, 60–62]. These include the broad immunosuppressant cyclosporine and targeted immunotherapeutic agents (e.g. dupilumab, fezakinumab, ustekinumab, gusacitinib) [16, 20, 35, 60, 63]. Due to shared features in their blood proteomic phenotypes, we were interested in determining whether therapeutics that have been previously utilized in AD may have any effect on the identified MDD inflammatory signature. Thus, using a similar approach to a previous publication in which we aggregated the molecular data from different clinical trials of AD [16, 20, 30, 60, 62, 63], we compared the relative effects of multiple therapeutic agents in AD on the MDD serum inflammatory signature in-silico (Fig. 3A). Overall, cyclosporine showed the highest consistent median percentage improvement in the MDD signature compared to baseline (week 2: 99.8%, week 12: 101.2%). Dupilumab showed a median improvement in the MDD signature of 58.1% at week 4, which increased to 112.9% by week 16, making it the highest of any of the therapeutics evaluated. Percentage improvements ranged from 40–80% for fezakinumab, ustekinumab, and gusacitinib at both the 40 mg and 80 mg daily doses. The analysis was repeated for gene products that were differentially expressed in MDD versus controls obtained from publicly available whole-blood RNA-sequencing datasets [37, 38], with similar results (Fig. 3B, C).

For the drug improvement analysis, the data for dupilumab were derived from a placebo-controlled, double-blind trial that evaluated the molecular lesional and non-lesional skin profiles of AD patients at baseline and after weeks 4 and 16 of treatment [20]. We next took the set of markers that were dysregulated in MDD and assessed their expression levels in the skin of AD patients from this trial at different treatment time points. The effects of both dupilumab and placebo on the MDD serum proteomic signature at weeks 0, 4, and 16 are summarized in Fig. 4. Overall, there was a cluster of markers from the MDD signature that showed elevations in lesional AD compared to non-lesional AD at baseline, and then showed incremental decreases with dupilumab therapy, with minimal or no changes in the placebo group. Predictably, the Th2 chemokines CCL13 and CCL17, which were elevated in both MDD and AD, showed significant decreases at weeks 4 and 16 in the dupilumab group, but not in placebo. Other markers that were part of the MDD proteomic signature that showed a trending effect with dupilumab (but not placebo) at week 4 and/or 16 compared to baseline included markers of T-cell/NK-cell activation or cytotoxicity (CD6, CD40, CD244, GZMA), Th17 (CXCL1, KYNU), negative regulation (CD274), inflammatory proteases (MMP9), and vascular or neurologic development, function and inflammation (SELP, F11R, TGM2, CTSB, HBEGF).

IL-4R antibody blockade in CSDS mouse models improved stress resilience

Finally, we tested whether, like the mechanism of action of dupilumab, pharmacologically blocking IL-4R in mice could prevent stress-induced behavioral alterations and promote resilience (Fig. 5). Applying a back-translational approach, we subjected mice to CSDS, one of the best-validated mouse models of psychosocial stress. It consists of the experimental mouse being subordinated by an aggressive CD-1 mouse through a combination of physical contact and sensory exposure over 10 days. Most stressed mice develop a behavioral phenotype characterized by social avoidance [64], impaired social reward [65], and reduced preference for natural reward (anhedonia) [64]. They also develop several physiological disturbances such as metabolic syndrome, systemic inflammation, and gastrointestinal disturbances [5, 66, 67]. These mice are termed stress-susceptible (SUS mice). However, a subset of animals shows behavioral and physiological profiles similar to unstressed control (CON) mice, which are termed resilient (RES). Since dupilumab was engineered to target the human IL-4 receptor alpha subunit for human IL-4Rα, we injected an anti-mouse IL-4R antibody to pharmacologically block IL-4R, or an isotype control daily, 20 min before the defeat bout throughout the 10 days of CSDS. This pharmacological strategy has been successfully applied previously in murine cancer research [68]. Mice that received an IL-4R antibody showed less social avoidance behavior compared to mice that received control IgG (Fig. 5A, B). The proportion of mice that were resilient after CSDS, based on SI ratio, was higher in the IL-4R antibody group compared to the IgG group (Fig. 5C). There was no difference in locomotion between the groups (Fig. 5D). In unstressed control animals, we did not observe an effect of IL-4R antibody administration on SI ratio (Figure S2A), time spent with or without the social target (Figure S2B) or locomotion (Figure S2C), suggesting that administration of an IL-4R antibody, compared to IgG injected mice, does not affect baseline behavior. Taken together, these data suggest that the IL-4R pathway is causally involved in the pathophysiology of stress-induced behavioral changes, and that blocking it could constitute a druggable target to treat stress-disorders [69, 70].

DISCUSSION

Here, we present the first study that compares the serum proteomic phenotype of MDD to AD and psoriasis. In contrast to MDD, where treatment options remain limited and focus primarily on pharmacological strategies targeting central nervous system neurotransmitters, recent advances in understanding the mechanisms of AD and psoriasis have led to the development of novel disease-modulating targeted therapeutics, such as dupilumab for AD [20, 35]. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating that the circulatory inflammatory MDD signature shares Th2 skewing and other immune and neurovascular abnormalities with AD, which may be suppressed by IL-4R antagonism as evidenced by both prior mechanistic studies of dupilumab in AD patients and our proof-of-principle, pre-clinical data that pharmacologically blocking IL-4R promotes stress-resilience.

We compared the blood immune profile of MDD to two inflammatory skin diseases that are mediated by distinct T-cell pathways. AD is primarily mediated by Th2, with variable involvement of Th1/Th17/Th22, while psoriasis is primarily Th17-mediated [35]. A recent meta-analysis has indeed shown an increase in CD4+ T-helper cells [9] and there is evidence of cytokine elevations in depression, suggesting that like AD and psoriasis, MDD pathogenesis may also involve preferential activation of T-helper cells. Our proteomic study illustrates significant up-regulation of proteins related to T-cell/NK cell activation and cytotoxicity (CD244, CD40, LAT), Th2 (CCL13, CCL17), Th1 (CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11), and Th17 (CXCL1, KYNU). Although Th1/Th2 imbalances have been reported in MDD, with Th2 generally linked to acute, mild stress while Th1 has been more associated with chronic, repetitive stress, the exact role of Th1/Th2 to MDD pathogenesis has been inconsistently described [71, 72]. On one hand, anti-inflammatory properties of Th2 were proposed, with enhanced depressive-like behaviors reported in IL-4-deficient mice [73], while mice with knockout in T-bet, a Th1-specific transcription factor, showed decreased depressive-like behaviors [74]. Contrasting studies showed that MDD patients exhibit increased levels of Th2 cytokines IL-4 and IL-13 and decreased IFN-γ [75]. Interestingly, Th2 cytokine production correlated with symptom severity in patients who also have atopy [76]. While Th2 was predictably highest in AD, we also observed elevations in both Th2 (CCL13, CCL17) and Th1 (CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11) in our MDD cohort. CCL17 was shown to be produced by hippocampal neurons in murine models and may be a neuromodulatory chemokine that regulates hippocampal synaptic transmission [77]. Multiple reports have linked Th17 activity to depression [78–82]. While several Th17 markers (CCL20, PI3) that were highest in psoriasis were unchanged in MDD, MDD also harbored a specific pattern of Th17 polarization with elevations only of KYNU and CXCL1. KYNU regulates tryptophan metabolism via the kynurenine pathway, where overactivation leading to the accumulation of neurotoxic metabolites has been associated with depressogenic effects on the brain and elevated risk of cardiovascular disease [83–87]. CXCL1 was shown to promote depressive-like behaviors and hippocampal apoptosis when overexpressed in mouse models [88, 89]. A previous Olink study also identified CXCL1 and other DEPs in our MDD cohort (CASP8, STAMBP, CD244, CXCL5) to be significantly associated with anxiety and depression in patients with chronic pain [90]. Overall, the immune profile of MDD with features of Th2, Th1, and Th17 polarization bears similarities particularly to the AD phenotype, suggesting the possible utility of repurposing systemic therapies from AD for managing depression.

In a direct comparison among various broad and targeted systemic therapies that were studied in clinical trials of AD, we found that the inflammation in MDD shows potential for modulation by medications used in AD to varying degrees. Predictably, cyclosporine showed the highest overall improvement in the MDD disease signature across the two time points tested at weeks 2 and 12, as given its broad immunosuppressive properties, it has been described as a criterion standard for reversing the inflammatory profile of AD [60]. However, cyclosporine is associated with side effects like hypertension and nephrotoxicity, limiting its use for long-term disease modification. In contrast, by specifically inhibiting IL-4Rα, dupilumab has a narrow mechanism of immune targeting leading to a favorable safety profile, as evidenced by its FDA approval for treatment of moderate-to-severe AD in patients who are ages 6 months and older. The greatest percentage improvement in the MDD signature at a single timepoint, surpassing even cyclosporine, was seen for dupilumab at week 12. Beyond the Th2 chemokines CCL13 and CCL17, several other inflammatory proteins that were elevated in MDD were observed to also be increased in the AD cutaneous transcriptome and suppressed after dupilumab therapy [20]. These include markers of T-cell/Th17 activation (CD6, CD244, CXCL1, KYNU) and vascular and neurologic inflammation (SELP, F11R, TGM2). While dupilumab directly modulates the Th2 axis, there may be additional downstream effects on other inflammatory features that could either be pathogenic or prognostically important in MDD. Notably, in pooled data from two phase 3 clinical trials of moderate-to-severe AD, dupilumab led to improvement in anxiety and depression as measured by Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale at week 2 with persistence through week 16 [91]. Similar improvements in depressive symptoms were also observed in other conditions, including asthma and prurigo nodularis [92, 93]. This improvement could be due to alleviation in disease symptoms but also may be due to the direct effect of dupilumab on the pro-inflammatory products that are dysregulated in these disease states. Thus, dupilumab, a specific IL-4R antagonist with a favorable safety profile that has been paradigm-shifting in the treatment of AD, could potentially be repurposed for the treatment of depression. These findings were further validated in a mouse model of CSDS, where pharmacologically blocking IL-4R during CSDS alleviated stress-induced social avoidance.

The systemic inflammation in MDD is further supported by elevations in products of apoptosis (CASP3, CASP8, STK4) that were observed in MDD, but lacking or diminished in AD and psoriasis [94, 95]. Several markers in the unique MDD proteomic signature are important to neuronal development, function, and inflammation, some of which were previously linked to depression and other psychiatric and neurologic conditions. For example, TGM2 was found to be increased in the prefrontal cortex of mice exposed to chronic stress and postmortem in depressed suicide subjects and has been identified as a potential therapeutic target in MDD [39]. AXIN1 is a scaffold protein that regulates neuronal proliferation, differentiation, and migration during cerebral cortical development, and has been implicated in autism [40, 41]. Increases in GLO1, which clears toxic byproducts from the CNS, has been associated with anxiety and depression-like behavior in mice with evidence of amelioration by GLO1 inhibition [46, 96, 97].

Previously, using the Olink analysis platform with a similar panel of proteins to assess the blood of AD and psoriasis patients, we identified many proteins involved in angiogenesis and vascular remodeling that have been implicated in atherosclerosis and cardiovascular morbidity and/or mortality [17, 35]. MDD shares some of these abnormalities with the skin diseases (IL-16, TNFSF14/LIGHT, SELP), but also harbors unique features of vascular inflammation (PDGFB, PECAM1, VWF). Additional examples of markers that were exclusively dysregulated in MDD include F2R, a thrombin receptor found on platelets and endothelial cells that promotes angiogenesis in response to stimulation by VEGF [47], LRPAP1, which has a role in LDL/VLDL absorption by endothelial cells and thus promotes atherosclerotic plaque formation [49], and ITGB1BP2, which is implicated in hypertension and compensatory muscle responses in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [49]. These findings suggest that like in AD and psoriasis, MDD may contribute to a pro-atherogenic state that confers an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, which may predictably be further accentuated in patients with greater levels of perceived stress. Overall, these data support previous work reporting differential expression of inflammatory markers in MDD (e.g. GM-CSF, IL-8, PDGF, IL-6, TNF-α), with individuals showing differences in cytokine reactivity to acute stressors and differences in cytokine/chemokine expression according to disease progression [98]. Furthermore, in line with our data, therapeutic response to antidepressants may be partly due to improvement of inflammation [99].

This study has a few limitations. Although Olink is a multiplex platform allowing simultaneous quantification of hundreds of proteins, it has decreased sensitivity to detect changes in certain cytokines below the limit of detection like IL-4 and IL-13. Our study also did not detect increases in IL-6 or TNF-α in MDD. Since only 25–30% of MDD patients exhibit chronic low-grade inflammation [100], larger sample sizes would likely be needed to reveal group differences in inflammatory markers with small effects sizes. Whether IL-4Rα inhibition, based on in-silico or actual clinical data, influences proteomic signatures specifically in the subset of MDD patients with elevated IL-6 remains an open question for future research. Additionally, data about depressive symptoms, which could further elucidate which blood markers may be associated with depression, were not collected from AD and psoriasis patients, who notably did not have any diagnoses of clinical depression. We also do not have clinical data on the number of past depressive episodes in any group. Furthermore, the potential effects of dupilumab on the MDD serum signature were only evaluated in a retroactive AD cohort with supportive data from a mouse model, while the actual clinical and in situ transcriptomic/proteomic effects of dupilumab on patients with MDD are still unknown. It will be interesting to investigate how IL-4Rα blockade influences the cellular and molecular mechanisms of both the peripheral immune system and the central nervous system, and how these changes underlie the observed behavioral effects. This study was not powered to detect potential sex differences in the proteomic profiles of the various disorders. Relatedly, the preclinical experiments were conducted exclusively in male mice, highlighting the need to test whether blocking IL-4R similarly promotes resilience in female mice. Finally, it is important to note that our study does not suggest that MDD and AD or psoriasis are pathologically overlapping nor that these shared markers themselves are the unique drivers of MDD or AD/psoriasis pathology. Instead, given the breadth of literature suggesting that systemic inflammation is common to both disorders and generally hastens disease progression, we hope to highlight that therapeutic attenuation of these proinflammatory pathways may improve overall health outcomes and minimize the disease exacerbation through inflammatory pathways.

In summary, the serum of patients with MDD contains unique up-regulation of neuroinflammatory and vascular inflammation or cardiovascular risk proteins, but also shares immune abnormalities with inflammatory skin diseases, most notably the common Th2 skewing, with AD. These immune abnormalities appear to be modifiable by IL-4R antagonism, as supported by a retroactive analysis in AD skin treated with dupilumab and in a mouse model of CSDS, although human studies and clinical trials are needed to further elucidate the pathogenicity of the Th2 axis in MDD. Our findings, together with the rapid expansion of targeted therapeutics that has revolutionized the treatment paradigm in inflammatory skin diseases, provide a rationale for employing a similar strategy in psychiatry, where specific immunotherapeutic targeting may lead to new personalized treatment regimens for MDD and beyond.
